Three kinds of tankless calls

Most tankless calls fall into three groups. Some homeowners want a new unit installed and need to know whether the house can support one. Some have a unit that throws an error code, runs lukewarm or shuts off mid-shower. Many simply need a flush because the heat exchanger has scaled up after a few years of service.

We handle all three, and we do not send you elsewhere if the unit is not brand new. If your unit has failed past repair, our water heater replacement page explains how replacement works, including swapping to a tank or a heat pump model when that makes sense.

Flushing and descaling: the service most units are missing

A tankless heater has a narrow heat exchanger that is very sensitive to scale. Mineral deposits coat the inside, the unit has to work harder to move heat into the water, and sooner or later it overheats, loses flow or throws an error code. Yearly flushing is the manufacturer-recommended habit for most units, and hard water makes it more important.

A flush uses isolation valves on the unit's hot and cold lines so a small pump can circulate a descaling solution, usually white vinegar or a manufacturer-approved product, through the heat exchanger for roughly forty-five to sixty minutes. The technician then flushes the unit with clean water, cleans the inlet screen and checks the condensate drain on high-efficiency models.

If your unit has no isolation valves, we can add them. That one change makes every future flush faster and cheaper. Our water softener and filtration page covers the next step if scale keeps coming back quickly.

Troubleshooting

Symptoms we see, and what they usually mean

Callers describe tankless problems in everyday terms, and the description usually points straight to the cause.

Water runs lukewarm

Scale on the heat exchanger, a partially clogged inlet screen, or a flow rate below the unit's minimum. A mixing issue from a crossed cold and hot line also does this.

Error code and shutoff

Ignition faults, flame rod fouling, exhaust blockage and condensate drain clogs are common causes. The code narrows it, but it does not replace a test.

A short burst of cold water

The cold water sandwich. A small slug of cold water arrives between two hot water uses, and a recirculation setup or a buffer tank can reduce it.

Unit will not fire at low flow

Some tankless units need a minimum flow before they light, so a trickle at a sink may not trigger them. A flow sensor issue shows the same way.

Is a tankless heater right for your home?

A tankless unit heats water on demand, so it never runs out as long as the demand stays within its flow rating. That rating depends on how much the incoming water has to be warmed. In winter, when the supply is colder, the same unit delivers fewer gallons per minute than it does in summer, so sizing should assume the coldest months.

Gas models usually need a bigger gas line than a tank heater, because they burn fuel faster while they run. They also need a dedicated vent, either stainless or approved plastic pipe for condensing units, with a condensate drain. Electric models need heavy circuits and can overwhelm a small electrical panel. Those practical limits, not the unit price, decide whether tankless fits a given house.

Tankless or tank: a straight comparison

Neither choice is right for every household. A family of five with two showers running at once needs a bigger unit than a couple, and a basement with no floor drain has a different risk profile than a utility room.

Where a tank wins

  • Lower purchase and installation price in most homes.
  • Simple parts and a familiar repair path.
  • No special gas line or venting upgrade in many houses.
  • Works through a short power outage on gas models without electronics, depending on the design.

Where tankless wins

  • Continuous hot water within the unit's flow limit.
  • Compact wall-mounted footprint.
  • Longer expected service life when flushed regularly.
  • No standing tank to leak onto a basement floor.

Installing a tankless heater in Vernon Hills

Before quoting an install, we check the gas meter and line size, the vent route, the electrical outlet for the controls, the space on the wall and the water pressure. For a swap from a tank we also check the existing shutoff valves, the discharge path for the relief valve and the drain for condensate.

The Village of Vernon Hills' published permit guide says tankless water heaters do not need a permit, while it describes a permit and final inspection for tank replacement. The guide is a few years old, and installing a gas appliance can involve its own requirements, so we confirm the current rules with the building department at 847-367-3700 before starting.

Our related water heater installation page covers venting, code items and location planning, and the same checks apply to a tankless project.
